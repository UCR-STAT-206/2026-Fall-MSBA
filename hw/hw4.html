Homework 4

Due 10am on October 29, 2026

Instructions

  1. The tip boxes titled What to expect describe what we found, so you can check your work. Because this assignment is open-ended, your numbers may differ if you made different, reasonable choices. When they do, explain why.
  2. Do not loop over rows with for or while. Use vectorized operations instead.
  3. Keep the raw data untouched. Load it once into blizzard, then do your cleaning on a copy called clean.
  4. This assignment is open-ended. In many places there is no single correct answer. What matters is that you make a reasonable choice, state it, and justify it in a sentence or two. Write as if you are explaining your work to a manager who will read the text but not your code.

Deliverables

  • A copy of blizzard_salary.csv to ensure your analysis is reproducible.
  • A Quarto source file named analysis.qmd, with code, output and written answers.
  • Any additional source files/modules you might create.

Overview

In August 2020, employees at the video game company Blizzard Entertainment anonymously shared their salaries in a voluntary survey, amid concerns about pay differences within the company. The data were collected from the survey. A copy is distributed by OpenIntro as blizzard_salary.csv.

Each row is one survey response. The columns are:

Column Description
timestamp When the response was submitted
status Full time employee or contractor
current_title Job title, typed in by the respondent
current_salary Current pay, in dollars, in the units given by salary_type
salary_type How pay is quoted: year, hour or week
percent_incr Raise received in July 2020, in percent (1.2 means 1.2%)
other_info Free-text comment from the respondent
location Office the respondent works in
performance_rating Most recent performance review rating

Your tasks:

  • Load and inspect the data.
  • Clean it so that salaries are comparable and categories are consistent.
  • Investigate the missing data and think about what can go wrong when you handle it.
  • Explore some relationships, and think carefully about what the data can and cannot tell us.

Part A: Load and inspect the data

  1. Load blizzard_salary.csv into a DataFrame called blizzard.
  2. Report its size (rows and columns).
  3. Report the data type of each column. Does anything look wrong or surprising?
  4. Create a Series called missing_counts with the number of missing values in each column.
  5. Use .describe() and .value_counts() to look at the numeric columns and at the categorical columns status, salary_type, location and performance_rating. In a few sentences, describe the problems you notice. These will guide your cleaning in Part B.
  6. Look through current_salary more carefully, sorted from smallest to largest, within each salary_type. Find any year salaries that look implausibly small and store the number of them in n_suspicious_salary.
TipHints
  • The file has no column of row numbers, so think about whether you need index_col.
  • Be careful with head(): the rows in this file are not in random order. What do you see in the first few rows, and is it representative?
TipWhat to expect
  • The table should have 466 rows and 9 columns.
  • Missing values per column: current_title 15, current_salary 56, percent_incr 44, other_info 297, location 3, and performance_rating 130. The other three columns (timestamp, status, salary_type) have none. If your counts differ, find out why before moving on.
  • We found 4 year salaries that look implausibly small (under $1,000). If you found a different number, take another look at how you sorted and filtered.

Part B: Clean the data

Create a copy called clean and keep all 466 rows in this part. Do not drop any rows yet. Each item below asks you to make a decision. State what you did and why.

  1. Suspicious salaries. Among the rows you found in Part A, some are placeholders (entries that cannot be real salaries) and at least one is a number that is real, but recorded under the wrong salary_type. Decide how to handle each kind, and fix it in current_salary / salary_type themselves. Explain how you told them apart.
  2. Locations. Standardize the location labels so that the same place is always spelled the same way. Look at the full list of labels and decide what to do with any that are not really a location, or that you cannot be sure about. Decide whether Los Angeles and Los Angeles Center Studios should be combined. Justify it.
  3. Annual salary. Create a column annual_salary that puts everyone’s pay on a yearly basis. You will need to state an assumption about hours or weeks worked. Say what it is, and say who it might be wrong for.
  4. Timestamps. Convert timestamp into a proper date-and-time column. Report the earliest and latest submission. Is anything about the dates surprising?
  5. Job titles. Titles are typed in by hand, and there are over 200 different ones. You do not need to clean them all. Instead, create a column job_family that sorts titles into a few groups (for example, engineering, testing/QA, art, design, game master / customer support, producer and Other). Decide on your own groups, and use .str methods on the lowercase title to build them.
TipHints for titles
  • Some titles could belong to more than one group (think of “Software Development Engineer in Test”). Decide how to handle these, and state your rule.
  • Report how many rows ended up in Other and how many have no title at all. Is your grouping missing something important?
  • A titles list such as clean["current_title"].value_counts() is a good place to start.
TipWhat to expect
  • You should still have all 466 rows.
  • After fixing the suspicious salaries, no year salary should be implausibly small. If you moved the mislabeled one to hour, you should have 64 hourly rows.
  • We ended up with 407 rows that have a usable annual_salary: 56 rows have no pay recorded and 3 more are placeholders. If your count is different, explain why.
  • Under a standard full-time assumption, annual salaries should land between roughly $20,000 and $250,000 (ours ran from about $28,000 to $217,000). Values far outside that range usually signal a unit mix-up.
  • timestamp should have a date-and-time type. The earliest response should be from 2020-07-31 15:02 and the latest from 2021-03-21 15:23.
  • No two location labels should differ only by capitalization or stray spaces.
  • Every row should have a job_family. In our rough version, about a quarter of the rows landed in Other. If yours is much larger, look at what is in it.

These are only a few of the things to look for. Some of your other choices (for instance, what you did with the 3 locations and the title groups) are not covered above, so be sure you describe them in your write-up.

Part C: Missing data

Many rows are missing something. Before you decide what to do about it, you should understand it.

  1. Make a table showing the number and percentage of missing values in each of the nine original columns of clean.
  2. The simplest approach is to drop every row that has a missing value anywhere. Try it. Store the number of rows that remain in n_complete_rows. How much of the data is left?
  3. Compare the full data with only the “complete” rows. Pick at least three quantities to compare (for example: the median annual salary, the share of respondents in Irvine, the share of contractors, the typical raise). In a few sentences, describe what changes and why this matters if someone only analyzes the complete rows.
  4. Look at the performance ratings and raises that are missing. Is the missingness spread evenly across employees, or does it concentrate in some groups (for example by status)? Look at the other_info comments for some of the rows with missing values. Do any explain why a value is missing?
  5. Pick one column with missing values (not other_info) and try to fill the gaps in, with a method of your choice (for example, a mean, a median, or a typical value within a group). Show how this changes the mean, the spread (standard deviation) and a relationship you care about. Then explain, in a short paragraph, why filling in is not as straightforward as it looks. Would you fill in these missing values for a real analysis? Why or why not?
  6. Possible repeats. Some rows look identical except for the timestamp, and in an anonymous survey we cannot tell whether they are one person who submitted twice or several people with the same job and pay. Find them, report how many rows are involved, and make and justify a decision about whether to remove them.
TipWhat to expect
  • Dropping every row that has a missing value in any of the nine original columns leaves 98 rows. Which columns are responsible for most of the loss?
  • 15 rows are exact repeats of an earlier row if you ignore timestamp (this is what .duplicated() reports). Counting the first copies as well, 27 rows are involved in total.
Note

There is no single correct answer to items 5 and 6. A strong answer says what was done, shows its effect with numbers, and is honest about what it assumes.

Part D: Explore relationships

Choose at least two of the following questions, or write your own. For each one, (1) state what you are asking, (2) provide a table or summary that addresses it, and (3) write 3 to 5 sentences interpreting it, including what you would be careful about saying.

  • How does pay vary across locations, job families or employment status?
  • Do employees with higher performance ratings receive bigger raises?
  • Who receives unusually large raises (say, over 10%)? Is there a pattern?
  • Did the people who responded later (for example, in 2021) differ from early respondents?
  • A question of your own that the data can answer.
Tip

You can compare groups by filtering to one group at a time, or you can look up groupby in the pandas documentation.

Part E: Reflection

Briefly, answer the following questions about the data as a whole. Explain in your own words.

  1. How were these data collected? Did the researchers (or Blizzard) assign anyone to a particular office, rating or salary, or did they just record what people reported? What does this mean for the kinds of conclusions you can draw? For example, could you conclude that a higher rating causes a bigger raise?
  2. Who is in the data, and who is not? Think about who chose to respond and who might have chosen not to. In which direction could this push the numbers (too high, too low, or no way to tell)? How might that type of bias mislead an analyst working with these data?
  3. What else could explain a pattern? Pick one comparison from your exploration where the groups might differ in more than just the thing you are comparing (for example, offices that do different kinds of work). Describe the other differences, and then check whether the pattern survives when you compare only people in the same job_family.
  4. What can’t you verify? Look at the columns and values again. Is there anything you would want to double-check with the people who collected the data before trusting a preliminary result?

Finish with a short summary of the two or three most important things a reader should take away from your analysis, including any limitations.
